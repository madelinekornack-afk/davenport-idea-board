Clark

Phil's personal data pipeline. Connect your email, your bank and card accounts, your files and your browser history once, and get one fast, searchable index of all of it that any agent, or you from the terminal, can use. Phil's name for it on Nov 11: a personal system of record. Every business queries one all day. No individual has ever had one.

Phil, Oct 22: "Here's all this data that has been all over the place that you've never really had access to all the time. Here's all of it. You can just take it in CLI. You can do whatever the fuck you want with it."

How it fits together

Everything it does

Built, in useEmail, searchableVectorized search over the whole inbox that understands context and semantics, not keywords. "Find the other emails where they talk about the same thing."
Built, in useTransactions, normalizedEvery charge resolved to the real merchant: a small classifier first, a bigger model with web search when it is unsure. About 94% accurate at Phil's.
Built, in useReceipts matched to chargesEmails feed the transaction pipeline as evidence, which is how Amazon and PayPal line items get itemized. Monarch users ask for exactly this and do not get it.
Built, in useFiles, classifiedStatements, K-1s, brokerage PDFs sorted and linked to the transactions and emails they belong to.
Built, in useBrowser history, searchableEvery page you have opened, in the same index as email and files, searchable by what it was about rather than by URL. Maddy's test case: "I was trying to find Sarah and Dan's wedding website and couldn't." Chrome's own history search only matches the title and address.
Built, in useOne authenticationEmail, Plaid and files connected once. Phil: "that is wildly sticky for everybody that does it."
Built, in useCLI firstEverything reachable from the terminal, no five plugins. Steve, by the end of the call: a trending "install this and your email is searchable" post would get him to try it.
Built, in useOpen to any agentThe index is the product, not a wrapper. Phil: "I don't want an assistant laid over top of it, I want pure unfettered access to my data, available to any system or agent I choose."
Built, in useProof: taxes in 90 minutesPhil filed with a K-1, wash sales and a closed Fidelity rollover. The system found the IRA distribution and then the account it came from, in email, from an account closed the year before.
Built, in useProof: FSA claimsMaddy's own version searched transactions plus email for every FSA-eligible expense and submitted them to HealthEquity.
NextMac and iOS appEmail and transactions in one GUI on top of the index, because people still want to see transactions, not just balances. Phil is building it now.
NextCatch-all desktop iconA box with screen-reading rights: "send this over to X and find the other emails where they talk about the same thing."
NextEvent graphWho said what, when, which decision replaced which. Phil's Nov 11 example: a revenue-share statement that is lighter than expected, checked across Slack, email, transactions and a spreadsheet, running in the background so it is ready when needed.
NextPrivacy as the masthead"We're not mining your shit." In the terms of service. Phil named it as the thing to lead with, and it is why Maddy does not use Claude in the browser for email today.

Where the group is on it

Phil's edge claimClark is one of the three places the group has an edge, "because I've already built a lot of it." The real edges, Orca and Media Studio, come from niche expertise; Clark's comes from being built.
Steve's pushback, Nov 11"Feels like infrastructure," drifting toward the personal-assistant space (Muse, Instinct) that is "mega crowded." The technical already have their own setups; the non-technical use Muse. And Anthropic keeps shipping the feature that makes this kind of startup obsolete.
Where Steve landed"Headless agentic apps: bring your own agent, we give you the backend," aimed at specific use cases. Every vertical AI startup has an ingestion pipeline underneath and a UI that locks you in; Clark is that backend without the lock-in.
Open questions for tonightWho pays first: the person who wants raw data access, or the one who wants the GUI on top? Which single problem area goes first (personal finance is the obvious one)? What does the version look like where Anthropic ships it next quarter? Is "headless, bring your own agent" the pitch or just the architecture?

What each piece earns today

Nothing on the App Store is Clark, so each chart is one thing Clark does and the apps that sell that one thing today. Appfigures estimates, US, iPhone, last 30 days (Oct 12 to Nov 10, 2026), net revenue. Download counts sit next to the name where modeled. Each chart scales to its own leader, so compare within a group.

Transactions is the only piece with real consumer moneyFive budgeting apps clear $350K a month on US iPhone alone, led by Rocket Money at $1.2M and YNAB, Copilot and Monarch at $580K to $870K. All at $100 to 200 a year. Roughly $45M a year of US iPhone revenue for the top five, before web and Android. Phil's Mac and iOS app would compete here, with receipt matching as the differentiator.
Nobody pays for the data layer by itselfThe lifelog and own-your-data group is empty: Limitless, Exist, Gyroscope and Saner have no US estimate at all. Steve's "it's infrastructure" point, in numbers. Money shows up only with a GUI on top (budgeting) or an assistant on top.
A narrow, expensive assistant can sellPocket, a paid "AI personal assistant," made $924K on 33K downloads, about $28 a download. Muse had 2.7M downloads for $0.8M, so Meta is giving it away for now.
Receipt matching and subscription audit are features, not productsThe two apps that actually pair receipts to charges, Expensify and Dext, make $22K and $10K on iPhone and sell to businesses. Subscription-cancel apps make $110K to $150K on weekly pricing. Rocket Money built cancel into a budgeting app and leads the category. Both belong inside the transactions product.
