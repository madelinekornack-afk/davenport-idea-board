The engine

Clark and Tally together: one place where your email, your money and your files are cleaned, classified and linked the moment they arrive, so anything asked later is instant. It is not sold on its own. Products sit on top of it, and the first one is for Orca.

Phil, Nov 11: "That is what every single business is. It is trying to make someone's high entropy environment that's a pain in the ass to work in into lower entropy. And then you sell that order to the customer."

In plain words

Every business you have ever paid sells order. A car is 30,000 parts sold as one thing. A property manager sells a calm month to an owner who would otherwise be chasing invoices. The engine is the machine that makes order out of a person's or a company's mess, and the mess today is email, bank transactions and files. Phil has been building it for himself under two names: Clark for email and Tally for money. On Nov 11 the three of you agreed that the engine is the thing worth building, that it is never the product, and that the products stack on top of it in a set order.

Why it feels different from Claude on its ownMost agents work at read time. You ask, and the model searches, scrapes and reads raw text for minutes. The engine works at write time. Every email and transaction is cleaned, named, typed and linked when it lands, so a question is a lookup, not an expedition. Steve's own version: "You have to solve the data layer before you can do the harness really well."
Why it is not a feature Google or Anthropic ships tomorrowPhil looked: Google does not vector-index free Gmail because the compute costs more than the account earns. A chat app would have to pull in all of your mail, money and files and store them. The engine does exactly that, for a price, on infrastructure that belongs to one customer.
Why not the health appPhil: "We all agree that a health app is out." Steve has Mercury for salary and Orca for the long game; a third context is impossible. Anything new must feed Orca, "two birds, one stone." The engine does: the first product is for Steve's customers.

How it fits together

The two halves, as Phil described them

RunningTally: moneyPlaid pushes each bank and card transaction into a pipeline the moment it posts. Stage 1 cleans the name. A fast model reasons over the cryptic descriptor and gives a confidence score. Low confidence sends a second model to the web, with account numbers and names stripped first, to find out that "paddle viewstax" is a software vendor. The result is saved as a machine-cleaned alias, and nobody is asked to confirm it. Amazon direct and Amazon Marketplace come out as different vendors. Stage 2 says what kind of money it is: a merchant purchase, Maddy sending Phil money, a card payoff, a transfer between accounts, a brokerage deposit or withdrawal. 47 economic types, none of which Plaid supplies. Phil hand-labelled 450 of his own transactions to tune it. Accuracy is 94 to 95 percent.
RunningClark: emailEvery message is ingested, vectorized and stored next to a keyword index, so search is meaning plus exact terms, not Gmail's Boolean operators. "Morning Brew contract" finds the 2023 thread with Devin Emery about pay, before any contract existed, even though the word contract is not in it. Clark also watches for receipts and passes candidates to Tally, which attaches the receipt to the exact transaction.
NextFiles, then the restA lightweight embedding model runs on the Mac or PC and indexes the local disk, which Spotlight cannot do inside PDFs and decks. After that, Slack and browser history. Health and wearables are a later layer, not a product.
RunningOne stack per customerEmail and transactions are the most private data a person has, so no customer shares a database. A CloudFormation template spins up a full isolated stack for each one; a prompt injection against SQL cannot reach anyone else's table. The team can still sign in through SSO to run it. Phil's own stack costs about $350 a month unoptimized, which is the number the $1,000 price has to clear.

The products, in the order you agreed

Statement reconciliation for property managers, on Orca firstThe scenario Phil walked through: an owner's bill is $10,000 above a normal month and the manager has to explain why. Today that is a scramble across vendor threads, invoices and tenant tickets. With work orders in Orca and email and payments in the engine, the explanation assembles itself: the furnace was serviced four times before it was fixed, here are the four invoices, here is the thread. Same machine for 50-owner month ends. Jason at WSPM is the guinea pig; Kyle at Green Street already queries Orca from Claude Code. ProFlow, the "AI property manager" Jason pays $1,200 a month, turned out to be a voice harness that had to build on Orca's webhooks to do anything about maintenance. This is board idea 2 and the Audit tab.
The executive system of record secondWhite glove, $1,000 a month, for the operators Maxinomics already reaches: people with several entities, real estate, deal flow, eight financial institutions and no order in any of it. Phil's number: 100 customers is $1.2M a year, with no funding. Steve's reaction to the price was the customer's: "Shut up and take my money." The pitch is privacy and portability. It is on your own infrastructure, we cannot see it, and you can take the data anywhere. This is board idea 1 and the Clark tab.
Tax season, pre-assembled thirdThe pain that started all of it. Phil spent an evening finding a five-year-old Fidelity 401(k) withdrawal to offset an IRS letter. Steve spent two and a half hours with Claude driving a browser through Schwab and old returns, stuck on login screens. A $1,500 CPA still makes you collect the documents. With a year of transactions and statements already typed and linked, the return is itemized before filing season opens. Board idea 7. Open question for December: a feature of the record, or the hook that sells it.

Who else is building it

SupermemorySteve found it on the night: "That's basically what's on our chalkboard." Sold to developers, billed by the token. Same architecture, different buyer.
GleanEnterprise search over a company's tools. The corporate version of the idea, sold to IT.
InstinctVenture-backed personal context. Nobody in the room has used it. Steve's point stands that he does not want a second app; he wants Claude to be the app, with better data under it.
Microsoft and GoogleHave the data, not the economics. Indexing every free account is too expensive, which is why Gmail search is what it is.
The platform risk, still openSteve's standing worry is that Anthropic ships the feature. The answer on the night was the architecture: write-time processing on one isolated stack per customer is not what a chat app ships, and the first customers are property managers who will never sign up for Instinct.
A second opinion, from Claude itselfSteve asked Fable whether the design was sound. It said the warehouse is "buildable with standard parts" and that no product gives it ready made, that it mostly helps questions about clients, operations and money rather than code, and it pulled four recent failures from its own lessons file that an index would have prevented: a misidentified site visitor, a wrong client price, a missed Green Street purchase, a question already answered in an unread email.
